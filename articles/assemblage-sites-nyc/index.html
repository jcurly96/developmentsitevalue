Assemblage Sites: Why Combining Lots Can Multiply Your Property's Value

By Jake Hulsh, BKREA

Some of the highest per-square-foot land sales in NYC aren't single lots, they're assemblages: two or more adjacent parcels sold together as one larger development site. If your property sits next to another under-built lot, understanding how assemblage value works could change how you think about selling.

What assemblage means

An assemblage is simply a group of adjacent lots combined, either physically or through simultaneous or linked transactions, so a developer can build one larger project across the combined footprint instead of several smaller ones.

Why combined lots are worth more than the sum of their parts

A wider, larger development site is usually more efficient to build on than the same square footage split across several narrow lots. One elevator core and one set of building systems can serve a much larger floor plate, unit layouts improve, and construction costs per square foot typically drop. That efficiency shows up directly in what a developer will pay: the combined $/BSF for an assembled site frequently exceeds what each individual lot would fetch sold alone.

Signs your property could be part of an assemblage

  • You're on a corner lot, or adjacent to one
  • Neighboring buildings are similarly under-built for the zoning district
  • A developer or broker has already approached a neighbor about their property
  • Your block sits in a zoning district that was recently upzoned

How assemblage sales are usually structured

Because an assemblage only works if every owner sells, these deals are typically structured with contingent or linked contracts, sometimes with options negotiated over time as a developer quietly assembles a position. This is also why an unsolicited offer on an ordinary-looking building sometimes signals something bigger happening on the block, worth understanding before you respond.

What this means if a broker calls about a neighbor's property

If you get a call about a nearby lot, or an unusually strong offer on your own property that seems out of line with a standalone valuation, it's worth finding out whether an assemblage is in play before you negotiate. Owners who understand the bigger picture are in a much stronger position than owners who only see their own lot.
